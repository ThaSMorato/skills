This is **explore**: widen what could be written, without committing to a structure. Committing is **exploit**, the job of `writing-beats` and `writing-shape`. Imposing an outline here closes doors before the material is in.

## The session
Interview the author relentlessly about the topic, the way a good editor would: ask for the example, the moment it happened, the thing they believe that others do not, the complaint, the sentence they would want quoted. Capture fragments from both sides of the conversation, starting with the very first thing the author says, the opening prompt included.

If no path was given, ask once where to save the file, and keep using it for the session.

## What a fragment is
Any piece of text that might survive into the final piece. The author has to be able to tell what it means; a cold reader does not. The bar is "is this good writing?", not "does this stand alone?". Fragments are deliberately mixed:
- a sharp sentence with no home yet;
- a claim with a one-line reason;
- a vignette: something that happened, a code snippet, a scenario, an analogy;
- a half-thought ("something about how X feels like Y; work it out later");
- a quote, a line of dialogue;
- a cluster of observations that belong together by feel;
- a confession, a complaint, a punchline;
- **a leading word**: one compact term that names the central idea, the way *tracer bullets* names a whole practice.

The leading word is the most valuable fragment to land: once found, it shapes the structure, the transitions and the title. When the conversation keeps circling an idea, push to name it.

## The file
```markdown
# <working title>

The first fragment.

It can run several paragraphs, and hold lists, code, quotes.

---

The second fragment.

---

> A quoted line worth keeping.

A reaction to it.
```

One `# ` working title at the top and nothing else above the fragments: no metadata, no table of contents, no date. Fragments are separated by `---`, with no headings, no tags, and no order beyond the order they arrived.

## Rhythm
- **Append without asking.** Say what you added in passing ("adding that"), and keep the conversation going.
- **Re-read the file from disk before every write.** The author may have edited, reordered or deleted between turns; their changes win. Append only, never overwrite. "Cut the last one", "make that sharper", "merge those two" are instructions to act on in place.

**Done when** the author says the pile is enough; then point at `writing-beats` (a journey through the material, beat by beat) or `writing-shape` (an argued article, paragraph by paragraph).
